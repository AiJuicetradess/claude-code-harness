# Registers

Genre-specific humor conventions. The main `SKILL.md` covers universal mechanics; this file covers what shifts when the speaker is a soca artist vs a trader vs a real-estate developer.

Each section follows the same structure:
- **Mechanic** — what comedic moves the register relies on
- **Voice rules** — what the speaker sounds like, what they never say
- **Genre-specific tells** — the failure modes specific to this register (in addition to the universal tells in SKILL.md)
- **Anchors** — slot for 2-5 hand-picked example lines from prior work, with one-sentence diagnoses

Fill the Anchors sections from your own catalog. Don't generate them — copy from work that actually landed.

## Calypso / Soca

**Mechanic.** Caribbean wit runs on four engines: *picong* (verbal sparring, often at named targets), *double entendre* (saucy meaning wrapped in innocent surface), social commentary disguised as fete, and *the chorus payoff* — the verse teases a setup, the chorus delivers a punch that gets sharper each repeat. Specificity to place is load-bearing: a calypso about "the city" is dead; a calypso about Port-of-Spain on a Friday at 4pm lives.

**Voice rules.** First-person, present-tense, low-status narrator who is in on the joke about himself. The speaker can name names — politicians, neighbors, ex-lovers. Never reverent. Never explains the double entendre. English/Spanish/Patois code-switching for emphasis, not for decoration.

**Genre-specific tells.**
- Generic "island life" imagery (palm trees, rum, sunset) — every tourist brochure already owns those words.
- Explaining the double entendre in the next line.
- Capital-R Romance — soca is rarely romantic; it's hungry, sweaty, and arch.
- Hortatory chorus ("come on everybody let's dance") with no specificity. The chorus needs a *thing*.
- Importing US pop-radio sentimentality into the verses.

## Reggaeton

**Mechanic.** Reggaeton humor is mostly *commitment-to-swagger* — claims so specific and unembarrassed they tip into funny. The mechanic isn't joke/punchline; it's a self-mythologizing voice that makes the listener grin because the speaker would never grin at himself. Specificity in brand, place, body, money. Code-switching does double work: rhyme density + insider signaling.

**Voice rules.** First-person, second-person ("tú"), never third. The narrator is winning. If he's losing, he's losing in a way that's still kind of winning. Brand names are concrete (specific watch, specific street, specific car). Refrains land via repetition with one shifted word per pass.

**Genre-specific tells.**
- Self-deprecation — kills the register. Soca self-deprecates; reggaeton doesn't.
- Vague "baby girl" / "mami" filler with no specific person attached.
- US trap clichés copy-pasted in Spanish (the genre has its own).
- Lyrical hedging ("maybe," "I think," "kind of"). Reggaeton commits.

## Bachata

**Mechanic.** Bachata is the serious-romantic genre of the bunch, so humor is rare and load-bearing. When it lands it's almost always *self-aware heartbreak* — the narrator knows he's a fool, names it, keeps loving anyway. Dramatic irony (listener knows what narrator doesn't) is the second engine. Specificity in domestic detail (the kitchen, the door, the phone she won't answer) beats abstract longing.

**Voice rules.** First-person, past or present, but never future — bachata doesn't look forward. The speaker is allowed to be pathetic; that's the point. Self-deprecation is acceptable but must be specific (not "I'm such a fool" — "I bought the wine she likes and drank both bottles myself").

**Genre-specific tells.**
- Bombast. Bachata is small-room music, not arena music.
- Trying to be cool. The narrator can't be cool; he can only be honest.
- Universalizing the heartbreak ("we all know what it's like"). The audience doesn't want a generalization; they want this specific guy on this specific night.

## Hip-hop punchlines

**Mechanic.** The punchline lives at the end of the bar; the rhyme word *is* the punch. Multi-syllabic rhymes carry comedic surprise because the listener hears the setup-rhyme pattern resolve in an unexpected way. Five primary moves: *double-meaning wordplay* (Lil Wayne, MF DOOM lineage), *threat comedy* (over-specific dominance as joke), *brag absurdism* (claims big enough to flip into humor), *the switch-up* (different cadence on the punch bar to signal the joke), and *internal misdirection* (the bar starts pointing one way, mid-line pivots).

**Voice rules.** First-person, present. Specificity in brand, neighborhood, opponent (real or rhetorical), instrument (specific gun, car, drug, watch — abstractions die). No question marks; bars assert. Punchlines never get a follow-up — the next bar moves to new material.

**Genre-specific tells.**
- Punchline followed by explanation bar — kills it immediately.
- Generic threat/brag ("I'm the best," "ain't nobody touch me") without the specific image that earns it.
- Borrowed punchlines (recognizable structures from other rappers). The genre punishes derivativeness fast.
- End-rhyme so soft it's not really a rhyme (e.g., "time" / "mine" / "life" — the laugh comes from rhyme-as-surprise, so weak rhymes neutralize the punch).

## Rock

**Mechanic.** Rock humor is *earnest-absurd* — commit 100% to a ridiculous premise and play it like it matters. Storytelling lyrics with twist endings are the second engine (think Warren Zevon, "Werewolves of London"). Self-mythologizing tips into funny when the gap between claim and reality is visible to the listener but not the narrator.

**Voice rules.** Can be first or third person. Narrator can be unreliable. Detail beats abstraction (specific town, specific brand of beer, specific year). The instruments can carry irony that the words don't — a deadpan vocal over a dramatic riff lets humor live in the gap.

**Genre-specific tells.**
- Trying too hard for "rock attitude" (eye-roll cliché territory).
- Self-conscious quirkiness as substitute for actual specificity.
- Comedy-band cadence (Tenacious D, Flight of the Conchords) when the project isn't a comedy band. Different register.

## Real-estate-with-personality (client real-estate voice)

**Mechanic.** The whole category is drowning in "paradise / oasis / slice of heaven / your dream awaits" — so any specific, dry, anti-brochure voice differentiates instantly. Three engines: *dry contrast* (Vancouver $200K math vs Las Terrenas $200K math), *insider knowledge* (the things only people who actually live in the DR know), and *anti-marketing-tone* (the seller who sounds bored of selling, which reads as high-status).

**Voice rules.** Never use the words: paradise, oasis, dream, slice of heaven, idyllic, breathtaking, stunning, pristine, hidden gem, off the beaten path. Use proper nouns — specific town, specific beach, specific developer, specific dish, specific person you'd actually meet. The speaker has lived there long enough to find the place funny. The reader is being let in on something, not sold.

**Genre-specific tells.**
- Any of the banned words above.
- Emoji decoration. Real estate that's confident doesn't use 🏝️.
- "Imagine waking up to..." opening structure. Anyone can imagine that; sell something they can't.
- Listing features in joke-cadence ("crystal waters? check. private pool? check. peace of mind? check."). It's a brochure wearing a comedy hat.
- Flattery of the reader's taste ("you, the discerning buyer..."). The reader knows.

## Trader / finance dry humor

**Mechanic.** The register is *understatement-with-jargon*. Matt Levine, Nassim Taleb, and the better corners of FinTwit run on four engines: *specific instrument references* (ZB, 6E, the front-month, the basis) as in-group signal, *anthropomorphizing markets* ("the bond market is mad at us today"), *self-implication* ("I am the dumb money in this trade"), and *counter-conventional-wisdom delivered flat* (the punchline is just the true thing, said without flourish, after the reader expected a flourish).

**Voice rules.** First-person, present, low affect. Never exclamation points. Never "literally." Acronym density is fine — it signals you're talking to people who know. The narrator has lost money before and will lose money again. The narrator is not impressed by his own thesis.

**Genre-specific tells.**
- Hype voice. The register punishes excitement.
- Emoji rockets, fire, money bags — instant amateur tell.
- "Massive," "huge," "insane" — finance dry humor downplays.
- Predicting with certainty. The register's humor requires acknowledged uncertainty.
- Explaining the trade after stating it. The in-group already follows; the out-group will Google.

## B2B SaaS / founder voice

**Mechanic.** Pain-point specificity (the screenshot, the exact workflow), category self-awareness (acknowledge the absurdity of the SaaS-tool space without breaking faith with your product), and founder-voice intimacy (talking to one person, not "users"). Comedy lives in not pretending the category isn't crowded.

**Voice rules.** First-person singular ("I built this because...") or first-person plural with a specific "we." Concrete product details, not abstract benefits. Never "revolutionizing" or "reimagining." Acknowledge the alternative explicitly when funny ("yes, you could do this in a spreadsheet, and I did, for two years, and it broke me").

**Genre-specific tells.**
- "Empowering teams to..." sentence-openings.
- Stat-first hooks ("82% of teams say...") — the register prefers anecdote-first.
- Naming the competitor as the joke. Cheaper than it looks; ages badly.
